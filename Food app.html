The Problem

Households generate 71% of the UK's 6.4 million tonnes of post-farm-gate food waste, yet individuals feel their personal efforts against climate change barely matter.

The Approach

A user-centered, five-phase design process grounded in two surveys, six interviews, and a competitive audit of five kitchen management apps.

The Outcome

A high-fidelity, usability-tested concept that reframes food waste as a daily, solvable behavior, plus a prioritized roadmap of next iterations.

Overview

A global problem, solved one fridge at a time

Climate change is one of the most serious concerns humanity will face in the coming years. Growing CO2 emissions, global warming, and rising sea levels all put strain on the existing population, resulting in displacement and competition for land and water. The paradox is that it is a worldwide problem produced by the actions of millions of people locally: individuals question what they can do to help, because their own efforts appear insignificant.

🎯

The design challenge: create something that helps people contribute towards addressing climate change. Our answer: focus on a sector where individual action has direct, measurable impact, household food waste.

Where we could intervene

A team brainstorming session mapped the major human-driven contributors to climate change. We chose food wastage because it is one of the few areas where a single person's daily habits create visible change.

Energy use Transport Industrial production Deforestation General wastage Pollution Food wastage · our focus

Project goals

Goal 01

Stay user-centered

Follow a user-centered design process and deliver an innovative, user-friendly solution to the problem statement.

Secondary Research

Understanding food waste

Food waste refers to any food products thrown away rather than consumed. While provisions exist to reduce it, the UK produces the highest amount of food waste in Europe. Typically, it falls into four categories and occurs across the entire supply chain.

Leftovers

Uneaten food created in households, restaurants, and food preparation.

By-product waste

Solid and liquid by-products from manufacturing, production, or agriculture, such as peels and trimmings.

Expired products

Products past their sell-by date in shops and supermarkets that can no longer be consumed.

Packaging waste

Unsold food that spoils quickly, plus waste generated from packaging.

Key insight: households are the single largest source of food waste in the UK (4.5 Mt). Individual behavior is therefore a powerful lever for change, exactly what the design challenge called for.

Root causes across the supply chain

Near the farm

Inefficient harvesting practices, out-of-date machinery, crop damage.

In transit & processing

Poor infrastructure, lack of cold storage, outdated equipment, lack of data to reveal hot spots.

Stores & restaurants

Poor planning and stock management, lack of food-handling skills, weak leftovers management.

🏠

At home (our focus area): lack of awareness about food waste challenges, poor storage and portioning, no shopping lists or menu plans, and poor understanding of date labels.

Listening to the people behind the waste

We combined quantitative and qualitative research: two online surveys to size the problem at the consumer level, six semi-structured interviews for depth, and a competitive analysis of five existing apps.

Qualitative research · 6 semi-structured interviews

Who we spoke to: university students aged 18 to 32, a mix of undergraduates and postgraduates. 4 interviews in person, 2 online.

What we asked about: meal planning habits, how often expiry dates are checked, what happens to leftover food, grocery shopping frequency, budgets, and the apps they already use.

Key insights from interviews

🧐 Managing expired food

Low awareness of the right way to handle items that have passed their date.

⏰ Expiration reminders

Users want frequent, proactive nudges as items near expiry so food gets used in time.

🛒 Shopping list sync

A convenient way to keep the shopping list in sync with the kitchen pantry while shopping.

🍲 Customized recipes

Quick, easy-to-make suggestions based on ingredients already available at home.

🎁 Donation awareness

Knowing nearby donation centres or communities where surplus food can be donated.

Ideas we deliberately discarded

🚫 Smell-detecting fridge sensor

A sensor inside the refrigerator that detects spoilage odours as a fail-safe once food passes its expected expiry. Why we said no: distinguishing normal food odours from spoilage indicators would cause false positives, and integrating advanced sensors would raise fridge prices, restricting access for many market segments.

🚫 Smart packaging sensors

Sensors embedded in food packaging that detect when an item is opened and log its expected expiry to the app. Why we said no: small durable sensors are technically hard and costly, data collection raises privacy concerns, and embedded electronics complicate recycling.

Our solution

Guardians of Food is a comprehensive mobile application that efficiently manages grocery inventory, proactively reminds users of expiry dates, and offers recipe suggestions and analytics to minimise both environmental impact and unnecessary expense caused by food wastage.

🥗 Health

Phase 03 · Ideate

Shaping the experience before pixels

We shaped the app's core elements by mapping user flows, structuring the information architecture, and visualising key journeys through storyboards.

User flows

We brainstormed flows on a whiteboard, mapping step-by-step user interactions visually. It gave us a space for dynamic exploration, quick adjustments, and collective input while refining the user journey. Below are the two core flows we settled on.

Flow A · Setting up your pantry after a shop

Open app → Buy groceries → Tap scan FAB → Scan bill → Items correct? → Confirm items → Pantry updated → Expiry reminders set

Flow B · Daily loop: a nudge saves food from the bin

Push notification → "Milk expires in 2 days" → Open recipe view → Cook suggested recipe → Mark item as used → Waste avoided 🎉

Information architecture

We accumulated our thoughts on a whiteboard first, then finalised the structure. Our focus: organising information so navigation feels intuitive and every key action is one or two taps away.

Phase 05 · Test & Iterate

Putting the design in front of real users

We ran moderated usability testing with four potential users to evaluate the interface, assess how usable and intuitive the design was, and collect actionable insights.

🎯 Testing goals

  • Validate the onboarding flow
  • Test the scan-items flow end to end
  • Test the My Kitchen flow and catch errors we had assumed away
  • Test the shopping list and recipe suggestion flows
  • Check whether users naturally understood the app's purpose without context (icons, typography, and more)

🧪 Method

Moderated, in person, 4 sessions using Figma's prototyping tool. Google Docs held the testing script and notes; Google Sheets organised the screener and results.

👤 Participants

4 participants (2 female, 2 male), aged 18 to 32. University students, comfortable with smartphones and everyday apps.

📋 Tasks

Participants completed four core tasks while two of us observed their interactions: create a shopping list · scan the bill after purchasing groceries · check that the pantry updated with the right items and quantities · view a recipe suggestion.

What we learned

👥 Shared shopping lists

Create collaborative lists for students who share groceries in accommodation.

📤 Upload, not just scan

Add an option to upload a photo of the bill alongside live scanning.

♻️ Tips for expired items

Add a dedicated section with ways to utilise food that has already expired.

🏦 Donation finder

Suggest nearby food banks or communities where students can donate unconsumed food.

Reflection

What this project taught us

🔍 Research before pixels

We spent generous time on research and brainstorming instead of jumping to implementation. It prevented us from over-committing to our first idea, and more importantly, from following the wrong idea extensively.

✂️ Cut what doesn't work

Not every process fits every problem statement and team. We learned to dismiss unhelpful processes early, saving both time and effort.

⚖️ Guidelines + intuition

Sometimes a design feels right even when it doesn't strictly follow guidelines. Balancing principles with visual judgment produced our most effective solutions.

🤝 Team craft

Near-daily check-ins, written meeting notes, and agreed next steps kept five people in different accommodations in sync, and helped us resolve creative differences quickly.

🧭 How we worked

We met in person at least three times a week and on Google Meet on other days. Every meeting began with a summary of completed work and our current stage, followed by next steps. We ended by agreeing how to connect next time and writing down the tasks to finish by then. This practice kept us organised and consistent.
